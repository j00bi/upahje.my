# Pricing the same dish twice

A dish sold at RM22.00 through a platform on a 20% commission nets the kitchen **RM17.60**. To keep the RM22.00, the menu has to read **RM27.50** — a rise of 25%, not 20%.

That gap is the whole difficulty of pricing for two channels, and the reason most shops get it wrong in the same direction: they add the commission to the price, discover the platform takes a share of the increase too, and stop there.

## The rise is the commission divided by what is left

The identity is short. If the platform takes a share `c` of whatever you charge, then the share of your price you keep is `1 − c`, so:

> price rise = c ÷ (1 − c)

| Commission | Rise needed to hold your net |
| --- | --- |
| 15% | 17.6% |
| 20% | **25.0%** |
| 25% | **33.3%** |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| 35% | 53.8% |

Those bands are not hypothetical: the Competition Commission's 2023 decision on foodpanda recorded commission ranges from 1–5% up to 15–25% across five anonymised franchised contracts. Whether a shop sits at the floor or the ceiling of that range changes its menu by sixteen points.

## On a real dish, with the packaging nobody counts

Take the RM22.00 dish, a 20% commission, and a packaging cost of RM0.80 that exists whether the order is delivered or not:

| | Price unchanged | Price rebuilt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Menu price | RM22.00 | RM28.50 |
| Commission at 20% | RM4.40 | RM5.70 |
| Packaging | RM0.80 | RM0.80 |
| **Kitchen keeps** | **RM16.80** | **RM22.00** |

At 15% the same rebuild lands at **RM26.82**, and at 25% at **RM30.40**. Notice what happens to the commission line as the price rises: it grows from RM4.40 to RM5.70. You are raising your price partly to hand over a larger fee, which is why the rise has to exceed the rate.

## Three dishes, rebuilt, and the drink that cannot be

The percentage is uniform and the effect is not, because packaging is a flat cost that sits on every order regardless of price:

| Dish | Own price | Packaging | Platform menu at 20% | Rise |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Small plate | RM12.00 | RM0.80 | RM16.00 | 33% |
| Main | RM22.00 | RM0.80 | RM28.50 | 30% |
| Sharing portion | RM38.00 | RM0.80 | RM48.50 | 28% |
| Teh O | RM3.50 | RM0.30 | RM4.75 | 36% |

The cheap dish needs the biggest rise, and the drink needs the most of all — a 30 sen cup on a RM3.50 item is 8.6% of the price before commission is even applied. That is why drinks and sides are where platform menus look most inflated, and why the honest response on small items is usually to bundle them rather than to reprice them: RM3.50 + RM0.30 across a 25% rise and a 20% commission leaves the kitchen with almost nothing after the flat costs are paid.

## The delivery fee is not yours to absorb

A shop watching its delivery orders lose money usually tries to fold the fee into the food. The published rates say that is the expensive way round.

On the observed RM4.70 delivery fee, folding it into the food price means the platform takes commission on it too: at 20%, you need **RM5.88** of extra gross to net RM4.70. At 25%, **RM6.27**.

Charging the customer RM4.70 directly, on your own checkout, nets you RM4.70 and costs the payment fee once — RM1.00 to RM1.25 on FPX, a flat amount, not a percentage. A flat fee is the better instrument for a small charge; a percentage is the worse one, and folding delivery into the food turns it into the worse one.

## Your rise meets their discount

Here is the part that decides whether repricing is visible at all. A shop that rebuilds its menu to RM27.50 is competing on a screen that can print a discount against it. Fifteen per cent off RM27.50 is **RM23.38** — so the customer sees a dish that is **6.2% more expensive** than the RM22.00 they remember, while the shop earns the RM22.00 it wanted.

That is the point of the rebuild: the discount stops eating margin and starts eating your buffer. It still looks like a price rise to the customer, because it is one — a smaller one than the commission, which is the best a shop can do without an audience of its own.

## Two prices, one kitchen

The structure that survives contact with both channels:

- **The platform menu is a cost-recovery price.** It carries commission, packaging, and the discount the platform expects you to co-fund. It is not a greedy number and it is not your price.
- **Your own channel carries the real price**, set from your own costs: food, packaging, payment fee, and nothing else.
- **The gap between them should be roughly the commission.** Not exactly — your own channel has its own acquisition cost — but near enough that a regular can see the difference and act on it.

If both menus read RM22.00, the shop is eating the commission on one channel and has handed the other channel no reason to exist.

## What a 25% rise does to your order count

Nobody in Malaysia publishes price elasticity for food delivery, so this article will not invent one. What can be said is where the risk lands.

A menu rebuilt to RM27.50 competes against a platform screen that can discount it back to RM23.38, so the customer does not see the full 25% — they see 6.2%. That gap is your buffer, and it is why the rebuild is survivable at all.

The test is two weeks at the new price, and one number: **orders per week, per dish**. Not revenue, which the discount moves around, and not the platform's dashboard, which counts visits you cannot audit. If orders fall by more than the margin you recovered, the price was too high for that dish and the answer is a smaller rise on that dish rather than across the menu. If they hold, the menu was underpriced before, and every month before this one was a subsidy you paid.

## A contract question we will not answer for you

Some platform agreements restrict what you may charge elsewhere, and this article will not tell you which ones, because that depends on a document only you have. Read the pricing or parity clause in your own agreement before printing two menus. If it restricts you, the honest alternatives are a smaller gap, a different channel — pickup, a group chat, a phone number — or accepting the fee.

What we can say is that the arithmetic above is the same either way. The clause decides what you are allowed to print, not what the fee costs.

## What to do, in order

1. **Rebuild the platform menu** using `c ÷ (1 − c)`, plus packaging, plus whatever discount you expect to co-fund. Do it per dish, not across the board: a flat RM3 rise is 25% on a RM12 dish and 10% on a RM30 one.
2. **Price the own channel from your own costs**, then check the gap against the commission. A gap much smaller than the fee means the own channel will not convert.
3. **Charge delivery as a line, not as a hidden markup.** A flat fee you publish is a number you can defend; a markup that pays a rider is a number nobody can see.
4. **Measure the owned share** — orders through a channel you control, divided by all orders. Below half, the platform is still buying your customers and the commission is the cheaper way to buy them.

## The rule

A shop with one menu is a shop paying commission invisibly. Two menus and a stated reason for the difference is the minimum a kitchen owes itself — and the only version of repricing that survives a platform that can discount your increase while you sleep.
