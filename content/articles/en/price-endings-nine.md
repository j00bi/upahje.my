# A 9-ending moves a new item, and not much else

The best evidence on 9-endings is three American field experiments, published in 2003, in which a $9 ending "increased demand in all three experiments". The lift was stronger for new items than for items the retailer had sold in previous years, and there is "some evidence" it weakens when a sale cue is already running. No effect size is published, and there is no Malaysian evidence at all.

## What the three experiments found

The paper is Anderson and Simester, *Effects of $9 Price Endings on Retail Sales: Evidence from Field Experiments*, in Quantitative Marketing and Economics volume 1, pages 93 to 110. The design matters more than the conclusion: price endings were manipulated in real stores, not in a lab, and the authors report two conclusions from the data.

First, the $9 ending raised demand in all three experiments. Second, the increase was stronger for new items than for items the retailer had sold in previous years.

## The effect is conditional, and the authors say so

A third sentence in the abstract is the useful one for a shop: "There is also some evidence that $9 price endings are less effective when retailers use 'Sale' cues." The hedge is the authors' own.

That reading fits how a price tag works. If a red sticker already tells the customer the price is low, a 9 at the end is a second signal saying the same thing, and two signals do not double the effect. The authors offer an explanation rather than a proof: 9-endings "may be more effective when customers have limited information, which may in turn help to explain why retailers do not use $9 price endings on every item."

## No percentage appears in this article

The abstract publishes no effect size, and the full text is paywalled, so the "24% more sales" figure that circulates with this paper's name cannot be checked here and is not printed. A directional finding is what the evidence supports: endings moved demand, with the conditions above.

That is the honest limits of a two-decade-old American result, and a Malaysian price decision made on it is an extrapolation.

## Malaysia has the convention and not the data

RM2.90 and RM19.90 are everywhere in Malaysian retail, which shows the convention, not its effect. No Malaysian field experiment on price endings surfaced in this pass, and no regulator publishes a retail price-ending study.

The zero in that table is the row a Malaysian owner should read twice. It is an absence, not a finding that 9-endings fail here.

## What a shop can do with this

1. Test the 9 on a new item, where the published effect was strongest.
2. Do not stack a 9-ending on top of a sale sticker.
3. Run the test for two weeks on comparable volume, and compare units, not revenue.
4. Keep the old price visible in the same test, because a price change and a price-ending change are two different experiments.
5. Do not treat a 9 as a substitute for a price the customer can justify.

My opinion, stated once: for a small Malaysian shop, the 9 is a test, not a policy. The evidence says it can move demand on something new; it does not say by how much, and it does not say anything about your customers. Spend a fortnight finding out rather than a year assuming.
