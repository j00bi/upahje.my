# When the platform is still the cheaper shop

Three checkout screens photographed within a week disagree about what dinner costs. The first, at 17:05 on 26 November 2026, ordering from an Ayam Gepuk branch on Grab, showed three lines. Subtotal, including tax: **RM20.90**. Delivery fee: **RM4.70**. Total: **RM25.60**. No service fee appeared on the screen, and it carried a note that the fee had been calculated at 17:02:16.

That single screen undercuts the strongest version of the argument this series has been making. The commission on that order is invisible to the customer, the delivery fee is not, and both are paid by somebody who never agreed to a rate card. This piece is the counter-argument, and it uses the same numbers as the six pieces before it.

## The commission buys five things, and this series only priced two

The earlier pieces costed the checkout and the audience. They left three items unexamined, and each one is a line the platform is charging for:

**The rider.** A delivery fee pays a person with a vehicle to move food across a city at dinner time, in rain, with traffic. A merchant doing that alone is hiring, insuring and dispatching.

**Disputes and refunds.** A customer claiming the order never arrived is a payment reversal, a conversation and a reputation decision. The platform absorbs it as a process.

**Demand smoothing.** The rider and the discovery are shared across thousands of merchants, so the platform can afford capacity at 19:00 on a Friday that no single shop could justify.

Two of the five have published prices, and the third has a visible one on the customer's screen. The remaining two are unpriced, and this article will not pretend otherwise.

## A shop at 40 orders a week, costed both ways

The abstraction is easier to argue with than a shop. Take one: 40 orders a week, 173 a month, every one of them delivered, a RM30 basket, and a courier who charges RM8 because the flats are far from the kitchen.

| Over a month | On the platform at 20% | Own checkout, own courier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue the customer pays | RM5,190 | RM5,190 |
| Commission | RM1,038 | — |
| Payment fee (FPX RM1.25 × 173) | — | RM216 |
| Couriers (RM8 × 173) | inside the delivery fee | RM1,384 |
| **Merchant keeps** | **RM4,152** | **RM3,590** |

Leaving costs that shop **RM562 a month**, and it has taken on the dispatch, the refunds and the phone that rings when an order is late. At the RM5 courier a city centre kitchen can find, the same shop is **RM13 a month better off** — which is to say, level, and carrying more work for the privilege.

That is the range the whole decision lives in: a few hundred ringgit either way, decided by one number the owner already knows, which is what their courier charges.

## The delivery fee is charged to the customer, and that changes who pays

Read the screen again. The RM4.70 is added to the bill, not deducted from the merchant's side. On that order the customer paid **22.5%** on top of the food, and the merchant paid commission separately — between RM3.13 and RM5.22 on that basket, depending on which band a contract falls into.

So the platform's revenue on a delivery order comes from two pockets, not one. That is why the comparison in the earlier pieces flatters an owned storefront: it compares the merchant's commission against the merchant's own delivery cost, and quietly leaves the customer's delivery fee out of the account.

Put it back. On a RM30 basket:

| | Own checkout, own courier | Platform at 20% |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer pays | RM30.00 + courier RM5–8 | RM30.00 + delivery fee (RM4.70 on the observed order) |
| Payment or commission | RM1.25 (FPX) | RM6.00 |
| Courier or rider | RM5–8, arranged by you | inside the delivery fee |
| Merchant keeps | RM20.75–23.75 | RM24.00 |

At the middle of the courier range the platform is **a ringgit ahead per order** and the customer is behind by more. On a RM20.90 basket it is further ahead, because a flat RM1.25 stops being small when the basket is.

## The delivery fee moves, and your own price cannot

One more detail on that screen, and it is the kind that ruins a tidy comparison: **"Delivery fee calculated at 17:02:16."** The fee is computed per order, from distance, demand and rider supply, and it changes as the evening does. The same basket at 13:00 and at 19:30 can carry different fees, and the RM3.00 the app offers off delivery is a subscription subsidy, so the customer next to you paid something else for the same food.

An owned shop should not imitate that. A flat delivery charge you publish — say RM5 within 5 km — is worse at surge and better in the quiet hour, and it is quotable, which the dynamic fee is not. The consequence for this comparison is that the platform's delivery number should be read as a median over a week, not as what one customer saw once. Ours came from a single screen at 17:05, and it is labelled that way everywhere in this piece.

## Run the counter with marketing as a variable, not a guess

The break-even identity from the arithmetic piece has one term nobody in Malaysia publishes: what it costs to acquire an order. Advertising rates exist, conversion rates exist, cost per acquired customer does not — not from a platform, not from a gateway, not from a council.

So print it as a variable. Solving for orders per month, with the RM999 annual gateway plan at RM83.25 a month, a flat FPX fee of RM1.25 and a 20% commission:

> orders = 83.25 ÷ (0.20 × basket − 1.25 − marketing)

| Marketing per order | RM20.90 basket | RM30 basket | RM50 basket | RM80 basket |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RM0 | 29 orders | 18 orders | 10 orders | 6 orders |
| RM5 | never | never | 23 orders | 9 orders |
| RM15 | never | never | never | never |

Read the "never" cells as the finding. **At a RM5 acquisition cost per order, a 20% commission on a RM20.90 or RM30 basket takes less than the payment fee plus the marketing would**, so there is no order count at which owning the checkout wins. At RM15 per order, almost nothing wins.

This is the sentence the other six pieces avoided: on small baskets with paid acquisition, **the platform is not overcharging you — it is subsidising you.**

## Three receipts, and the price moved 52 points

Within a week, three checkout screens, three platforms, three different answers to what dinner costs:

| | Grab | Shopee Food | foodpanda |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Food bill | RM20.90 | RM14.90 | RM22.00 |
| Delivery | RM4.70 charged | free | RM5.99, waived |
| Discount | — | RM4.49 | RM3.30 |
| Fees | none shown | none shown | RM0.65 platform, RM0.05 tax |
| **Customer paid** | **RM25.60** | **RM10.41** | **RM19.40** |
| Against the food bill | **+22.5%** | **−30.1%** | **−11.8%** |

That is a 52-point spread on the same kind of order in the same city in the same week. No shop sets prices that way, and no shop can: the customer-facing price here is the output of a subsidy auction between platforms, and the merchant's side of it is invisible on all three screens.

Two details are worth more than the totals.

**The discounts all look like round promotions.** Shopee's item discount was 10% of its subtotal, its voucher a flat RM3.00, foodpanda's discount **exactly 15% of RM22.00**. Fifteen per cent is also the floor of the 15–25% band recorded in the regulator's foodpanda decision. That may be a coincidence of pricing psychology; the screens do not say who funds any of it, and this article does not guess. What it can say is what the arithmetic looks like if the shop funded both its discount and a 15% commission: on the foodpanda order, **RM3.30 + RM3.30 = RM6.60 on RM22.00, a 30% take** — and the customer still paid RM9.29 less than the menu price suggested.

**A missing fee line is not a missing fee.** Grab's screen showed no service fee. foodpanda's showed a platform fee and a service tax as separate lines, RM0.70 together, small enough that most customers read past it. Two platforms, one of which itemises and one of which does not — and the commission, the largest number of all, is itemised by neither.

None of this decides whether to stay. It decides what you are competing against. A shop comparing its own prices to a platform's price list is comparing itself to the output of an auction it cannot see.

## The 8% SST nobody adds

CHIP prints one line under its rate table that most comparisons drop: **"Transaction fees are subject to 8% SST."** An FPX payment at RM1.00 is RM1.08 charged. Billplz's page shows no such line, so whether RM1.25 includes tax is unresolved — and on a small basket the difference is not decorative.

Put the 8% on the RM1.25 this table uses and the fee becomes RM1.35. At a 20% commission the RM20.90 basket moves from 29 orders to 30, and the RM30 basket stays at 18. The SST does not change the argument; it does change the answer enough that a shop sitting on 29 orders should count again.

## Four shops that should stay where they are

1. **Under about 25 orders a week.** Fixed costs do not care about your volume. At 100 orders a month the gateway plan alone is 83 sen an order before a single fee is paid.
2. **A single-product shop.** If the business is one dish done properly, there is no menu breadth to make discovery cheaper, and discovery is what you are buying.
3. **No audience at all.** Zero followers, no group chat, no regulars who message directly. The owned share is 0%, and the discovery term has no counterweight.
4. **A shop whose whole point is delivery radius.** If the food only works warm within 6 km, the rider is the product.

None of these are failures. They are the ordinary state of a shop in its first year.

## Four shops that should leave

1. **Repeat customers above half of orders.** Their discovery is already done, and you are paying commission on customers who would have found you anyway.
2. **Pickup-heavy.** No rider to buy, no delivery fee to argue about, a flat RM1.00–1.25 fee against a commission that scales with the bill.
3. **Baskets above RM80.** Commission is a percentage and the fee is flat, so the crossover moves fast: 6 orders a month breaks even at RM0 marketing, 9 at RM5.
4. **An existing following.** mejamakan's 16,000 Instagram followers are not orders, but they are the channel that makes the discovery term small enough to solve.

## The third answer is the real one: run both

Almost no shop should do all of one or all of the other, and the arithmetic says so once the marketing line is in it. The shape that survives:

- **The platform keeps delivery and new customers**, because those are the two things it is genuinely cheaper at.
- **Your own checkout takes the regulars and the pickup**, where the commission is pure loss.
- **One kitchen, two order queues.** That is the real cost, and it is a person's attention rather than a fee.

The number that tells you whether it is working is the share of orders arriving through a channel you own. Below half, the platform is still doing the work and its commission is the cheaper way to buy it. Above half, it is a delivery contractor with a fee you can negotiate.

## How to count your owned share in two weeks

The half rule is only useful if the number is measured rather than guessed. Two weeks is enough:

1. **Tag every order by how it arrived.** Platform, own site, direct message, repeat customer, walk-in. Five categories, one tally sheet, no software.
2. **Write down what you spent on the owned channels**, including your own hours at a rate you would pay someone else. A free channel that eats six evenings a week is not free.
3. **Divide** the owned orders by the total, and the spend by the owned orders. You now have the two numbers this entire series circles: your owned share, and your acquisition cost per owned order.

If the acquisition cost lands above RM5 and the share is under half, the platform is cheaper and the arithmetic above says so. If the share is over half and the cost is under RM5, the commission is a fee for customers who already know you.

## Where this series was right, and where it was optimistic

Six pieces argued one direction. Read against the table above, here is what survives and what does not.

- **"An FPX payment costs RM1.25 and a commission costs 15–25%."** Survives. Both are published or recorded, and neither has moved.
- **"At delivery the platform is competitive."** Survives, and it was the finding that made this piece necessary.
- **"The storefront is solved; the audience is the work."** Survives, with a correction: the audience is not just work, it is **a cost that lands on the same line as the fee**, and once it exceeds about RM5 an order the storefront stops being cheaper.
- **"One person can build and run this."** Survives technically — husnieats did — but a prototype with three dishes is not a shop at 173 orders a month. The build is cheap; the operating is not.
- **"Half your orders from a channel you own is the number to watch."** Survives, and it gains a partner: the acquisition cost per owned order. One without the other is half a measurement.

That is the whole correction, stated in one paragraph so nobody has to hunt for it: we were not wrong that owning the shop got cheap, we were quiet about the marketing line, and the marketing line is where small shops lose.

## What would change our mind

Three artefacts, each checkable:

1. **A platform publishing a merchant rate card.** Every commission figure in this series comes from a regulator's decision about five anonymised contracts, because no platform prints one.
2. **A Malaysian number for acquisition cost per order**, by category, with a date. The table above has an empty column until somebody measures it.
3. **A gateway bundling discovery with payments.** If a payment provider starts delivering customers, the commission's second service gets a competitor and the whole comparison reopens.

## The wager

Here is what we will say plainly, having spent a series arguing the other way: **for a shop under about 100 orders a month, on baskets under RM40, the platform is the cheaper shop, and leaving it to save the commission is a way to lose money slowly.**

That is a wager, not a prophecy. The day a merchant shows us a rate card that says otherwise — or a Malaysian source gives that acquisition number and it comes in under RM3 — we will publish the correction and link it here.

What we will not do is print a cost per customer we have not seen, or tell anyone to leave a channel that is still filling their tables.
