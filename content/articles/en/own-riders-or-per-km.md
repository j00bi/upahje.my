# Own riders or per-km couriers: the radius where it breaks

Five delivery services were checked for this piece, and not one of them publishes a per-kilometre tariff a shop can plan from. GrabExpress renders but prints no fare, only two add-on prices; Lalamove's pricing page 404s and the fare lives in a configurator; Pos Laju renders its tariff client-side; J&T and Ninja Van return 404 on the pages tried. The radius where your own rider beats a courier is therefore not a published number — it is arithmetic you run on your own quotes.

## What the five pages actually publish

The table records what each service printed, and one column for what it is, because these five are delivery services and nothing else — no regulator, no council, no agency sits in the same column.

| Service | What the page published | Figure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GrabExpress | no fare in the served text; add-on prices only | RM0.80 and RM1.30 |
| Lalamove | nothing; the fare needs the quote configurator | — |
| Pos Laju | nothing in the served text; the tariff is client-rendered | — |
| J&T Express | nothing; pricing page returned 404 | — |
| Ninja Van | nothing; pricing page returned 404 | — |

The two GrabExpress figures are add-ons, not a fare: that is what the page prints, and calling them a delivery price would be the overclaim this table exists to prevent.

## Why a per-km tariff would not settle it anyway

Even if one of the five printed a rate card, it would be a rate for their model: a base fee, a distance band and a surcharge schedule. Your own rider's cost is a different shape — a monthly wage, a bike or a car, insurance, fuel and the hours that rider spends idle between orders.

So the comparison is not per kilometre against per kilometre. It is **cost per delivery** against **cost per delivery**, and each side is built from different units.

## The two lines to write down

For a courier, the line is the quote at the distance you actually deliver over. Ask each service for a quote at three distances — your shortest, your median and your furthest — and write the three numbers down.

For an own rider, the line is:

`cost per delivery = (rider monthly cost + vehicle + insurance + fuel) / deliveries per month`

The denominator is the number that decides everything. A rider paid for a full month and given 100 deliveries a month carries that whole monthly cost on 100 orders; the same rider on 600 deliveries carries it on 600.

## The radius where it turns

Run both lines at your shortest and furthest distances. The radius where owning turns is the distance at which the courier's quoted cost per delivery passes your rider's cost per delivery.

Three things move that point:

1. **Volume.** The rider's monthly cost is fixed, so more deliveries per month pull the crossover radius inward.
2. **Basket size.** A bigger basket tolerates a bigger delivery cost, which pushes the crossover outward.
3. **Return trips.** A courier charges for the trip out; your rider's return leg is unpaid time you already own.

There is no published number for any of the three in Malaysia, and this article does not invent one. The arithmetic needs your wage, your vehicle cost and three quotes.

## What to ask a courier before comparing

1. The quote at your shortest, median and furthest distance, in writing.
2. Whether the quote includes fuel, tolls and waiting time.
3. What a failed delivery costs and whether it is charged again.
4. Whether the rate changes at a volume band.
5. Whether the service covers your postcodes at all.

## What to do this month

1. Write down your three distances and the number of deliveries on each.
2. Get three written quotes per distance from at least two services.
3. Total your rider's monthly cost, including the hours they are not delivering.
4. Divide by deliveries per month and compare with the quotes.
5. Choose per distance band, not once for the whole business.

A shop does not need one delivery answer. It needs a radius inside which the rider wins and a service that takes everything beyond it.
