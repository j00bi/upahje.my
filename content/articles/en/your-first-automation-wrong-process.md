# Your first automation should not be your worst process

*This is an opinion piece. It argues a position from what we have seen reading and running these systems; it does not report a measurement.*

Most Malaysian SMEs automate the wrong process first. Not because they pick badly on price, but because they pick the process that annoys the owner most, and that process is usually the one nobody has ever written down. Automating it buries the mess under a tool instead of fixing it.

## Why is the worst process the wrong starting point?

Because the thing that makes a process painful is almost never the typing. It is the exceptions.

Take the invoice chase every small business has: a supplier sends the wrong quantity, the delivery order and the invoice disagree, someone calls to confirm, and the call produces a note in someone's WhatsApp. The typing is trivial. The knowledge of what to do when the numbers disagree is the whole job, and it lives in one person's head. Automate the typing and you have automated the cheap part while the expensive part gets quieter and harder to find.

## Argument one: automation removes the evidence

A manual process leaves a trail. Emails, scribbles, a correction someone made at 4pm on a Friday. Ugly, but readable, and it tells you where the exceptions were.

An automated process leaves a log, and the log only contains what you thought to log. When the first wrong invoice goes through, the investigation starts from what the tool recorded, which is usually the fields it knew about. The exception lives outside that.

There is a workable version of this: log the manual process for two weeks before touching it. A sheet with one row per instance, the time it took, and a column for what went wrong. That sheet is worth more than any automation quote, and it costs nothing. The [rates article](https://upahje.my/en/articles/automation-rates-malaysia-published) assumes you know your volume before you ask for a price; this is how you find out.

## Argument two: the first automation is a test, not a fix

The first one teaches your team how to buy, brief, test and maintain automation. Those are four separate skills, and a painful process is the worst training ground for them because failure is expensive and the person who understands it has no spare time.

Start with something boring. A weekly export that a junior copies between two systems. If it goes wrong, nobody's month is ruined, and you learn what a failed run looks like in your own logs. The [piece on telling a demo from something that keeps running](https://upahje.my/en/articles/what-an-ai-agent-can-do) is easier to apply when the stakes are low.

## Argument three: the worst process is often the wrong process

Pain is a signal that the process has accumulated exceptions, and exceptions usually mean the process is compensating for something upstream. The invoice mismatch is not an invoicing problem. It is a receiving problem, or a supplier problem, or a quoting problem.

Automating a compensating process locks the compensation in. Now you have a tool-shaped reason not to fix the thing that caused it. I have seen this argued in reverse as "automate first, fix later", and it only works if the thing you automate is the source rather than the symptom.

## What should you do instead?

Three steps, and none of them needs a purchase.

1. **Write the process down**, in the order it happens, including the parts that are not steps: "ask Aminah because she knows the supplier".
2. **Track two weeks of it**, one row per instance, with a column for what went wrong. Two weeks is enough to see the shape.
3. **Pick the second most annoying process**, not the first. By then you know what a good brief looks like, and the process you avoid is the one with the most hidden exceptions.

If you want the sharper version: instrument, don't automate, until the exceptions are written down. Then automate the thing with a clear finish line and a cheap mistake, which is usually the boring process nobody complains about.

## Where I could be wrong

If the process is high volume, low variation and genuinely a copy-paste job, automating it first is fine, and waiting for two weeks of data is a waste of time. The argument here is about the process that generates complaints, not the one that generates rows.

And there is a real cost to waiting. Two weeks of tracking is two weeks of the pain continuing, and for some businesses that is not affordable. The honest version is that this is a trade between knowing more and suffering longer, and only the owner can price it.

## FAQ

### So should I never automate a painful process?

Automate it after you have written down its exceptions. The pain is evidence that the exceptions exist, so the process is a fine candidate, just not a fine first candidate.

### What if we have no time to track anything for two weeks?

Track the failures only. One line whenever something goes wrong, with what happened and who fixed it. That takes minutes and still shows you where the work really is.

### What makes a good first automation?

A process with a clear finish line, a cheap and visible mistake, and someone who owns it on the other side. Boring is the point: the first one is where you learn the buying and the maintenance, not where you make the annual saving.
