# The storefront is a checkout, not an audience

Owning your shop changes who takes the payment. It does not change who finds you. A platform sold two things in one invoice: the software that takes an order, and the customers who arrive without you paying for them. The software is now cheap — RM1.25 an FPX payment — and the customers were never software at all.

## The commission was buying discovery

Read the commission as a fee for two services. The first is a checkout, and a checkout is now a published rate. The second is a customer who opens the app already looking for food.

That second service is what disappears the day you own the storefront. Nothing on your own site introduces you to someone who has never heard of you, and no gateway fee includes an audience.

## The marketing term your equation was missing

The break-even arithmetic has a hole in it, and the hole is marketing. Written out:

`commission × basket × orders = payment fee × orders + fixed cost + marketing`

Move the terms and the order count changes. A shop that ignores the marketing line solves an equation that does not apply to it.

There is no published rate for that line in Malaysia. No platform, gateway or council publishes what it costs to acquire a food customer, and this article will not invent one. What it can say is where the line comes from: the channels you build yourself.

## What is published, and what is not

| What the platform provided | What is published | What owning costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Checkout | RM1.25 per FPX transaction on Billplz Basic, RM0.75 on Standard; cards 1.8% or 1.5% | the same fee, paid to the gateway |
| Settlement | next business day for FPX, T+2 for cards | the same terms |
| Discovery | nothing: commission bands from 1% to 25% recorded for five anonymised franchised merchants in a September 2023 decision (n=5) | no published rate anywhere |

The third row is the one that decides the series. Everything the platform charged for except discovery can now be bought at a published price. Discovery cannot.

## What an owned audience looks like when it works

mejamakan, a lauk delivery business in KL and Selangor, runs its own site rather than a platform page. On 26 November 2026 its site showed a weekly menu, accounts and ordering on its own domain, "from RM18/meal", weekday delivery and a renewing plan, with Instagram at **16K followers** as the front door.

That follower count is the observable part, and it is also the honest limit of this article: followers are not orders, and Instagram does not publish a conversion rate. What the example shows is where the audience lives — a channel the shop controls — not what it cost to build.

## Half is the number to watch

One number tells you whether the swap has worked: **the share of your orders arriving through a channel you own**. Direct messages, repeat customers, the site itself, the group chat.

Below half, the platform is still doing the discovery and its commission is the cheaper way to buy customers. Above half, the platform is a delivery contractor and the commission conversation changes.

## What to do this month

1. Count last month's orders by channel, and put a number on the owned share.
2. Count what you spent on the owned channels, including your own hours at a rate you would pay someone.
3. Put both into the equation above and solve for orders.
4. Compare that count with the count the platform needs to beat.
5. If the owned share is below half, keep the platform and grow the channel; if above, renegotiate the commission or leave it.

The shop is the easy half and the audience is the business. Anyone selling you a storefront as if it came with customers is selling the checkout and calling it a market.
