# Owning the shop means inheriting the discovery problem

A marketplace sells two things in one invoice, and only one of them is software. The first is the checkout that takes the order. The second is the customer who opens the app already looking for food. The checkout has become cheap enough to buy on a published rate; the customer was never software, and no gateway fee includes one.

That is the trade this series weighs, and it is not a slogan in either direction. Owning the shop replaces a percentage with a fixed bill, which is good, and replaces someone else's audience with your own job, which is expensive. The arithmetic below says where the first advantage is large enough to pay for the second.

## The marketplace sold a checkout and an audience

Two services sit inside one commission. The first is a checkout: an order form, a payment, a receipt. The second is discovery: a listing where a hungry person who has never heard of you can find you, compare you and order.

Only the first can be bought at a published price today. An FPX payment costs RM1.00 at CHIP or RM0.75 to RM1.25 at Billplz depending on the plan, and it settles next business day. A card payment costs a percentage — 2.0% at CHIP, 3% plus RM1.00 at Stripe — and settles in two business days at CHIP and Billplz, though Stripe pays out on a rolling, weekly or monthly setting, with CHIP noting that its transaction fees carry 8% SST.

Discovery has no published price anywhere in the pages read for this series. No platform, gateway or council prints what it costs to acquire a food customer, and this article will not invent one.

## The two fee shapes, and why the difference is the decision

A flat fee and a percentage behave in opposite directions as the basket grows. That single fact decides which payment method suits which shop, and it is the first arithmetic a new storefront should run.

| Gateway | Method | Fee as printed | Settlement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CHIP | FPX B2C | RM1.00 | every next day |
| CHIP | Local credit card | 2.0% | every 2 business days |
| CHIP | Local debit card | 1.0% | every 2 business days |
| Billplz | FPX B2C | RM1.25 Basic, RM0.75 Standard | next business day |
| Billplz | Card, MYR | 1.8% Basic, 1.5% Standard | T+2 business days |
| Stripe | Domestic cards | 3% + RM1.00 | rolling, weekly or monthly |

On a RM30 basket, CHIP's FPX fee is RM1.00 and its card fee is RM0.60 before SST, about RM0.65 with it. The card is cheaper per transaction at that basket. On a RM150 basket, FPX stays at RM1.00 and the card fee is RM3.00, RM3.24 with SST. The flat fee stops mattering; the percentage does not.

Settlement is a property of the method, not the gateway. The same provider prints next-day for FPX and two business days for cards, and Stripe's payout is a setting — rolling, weekly or monthly — rather than a term. A shop that plans cash flow from a headline "X days" figure is planning from the wrong line.

## The commission is the only number nobody prints

Neither delivery platform publishes a merchant commission. The only regulator-recorded figures are the bands in the Malaysia Competition Commission's foodpanda decision of 11 September 2023: 1% to 5%, 10% to 25%, 15% to 20%, and twice 15% to 25%. Those are **five anonymised franchised merchant contracts (n=5)**, not a platform's current rate card, and any sentence that presents them as today's price is wrong.

They are still the only numbers a reader can start from before a contract lands, so the arithmetic below uses the low, middle and high edges and lets the count move.

## What a weekend storefront solves, and what it does not

A storefront is not a hard build. In a weekend a shop can have a domain, a menu with prices, a way to take money that does not require a gateway, an order notification and a receipt, and none of that requires a developer or a subscription to a platform. That is the "easy half" this series keeps naming, and it is genuinely easy.

What the weekend does not produce is a person who has never heard of the shop typing the domain. The build ends with a working checkout and zero visitors, and the second condition is the one that decides whether the shop earns anything.

Put plainly, the capability is a list and the non-capability is one sentence:

- **You can have** a domain, a page per dish, a payment method, a confirmation, and a record of the order.
- **You cannot get** the stranger, the search result, the recommendation or the repeat customer to arrive, because those are earned by marketing, by habit and by time.
- **You can own** the customer list once someone orders.
- **You cannot borrow** the platform's discovery once you leave it.

A shop that reads only the first bullet will build the checkout and wonder why the phone is quiet. A shop that reads the last two understands why the arithmetic below has a marketing term in it.

## The break-even table

The equation has two sides. The platform takes a percentage of the basket. Your own checkout takes the payment fee, a fixed monthly cost and — the term everybody leaves out — the marketing that now replaces the platform's discovery.

`commission × basket × orders = payment fee × orders + fixed cost + marketing`

The table solves the fee side only, with a RM150 monthly cost and a RM30 average basket as stated assumptions. Substitute your own numbers and read down the columns.

| Commission band | FPX at RM0.75, orders a month | FPX at RM1.25, orders a month | Card at 1.8%, orders a month |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5% | 200 | 600 | 157 |
| 15% | 40 | 47 | 38 |
| 25% | 23 | 24 | 22 |

At the top of the regulator's widest band, owning beats the commission after about **23 orders a month**. At the bottom of the narrowest band it takes **200** on the cheapest FPX plan and **600** on the free one. That spread is the whole argument: the commission rate decides whether owning the shop is a rounding error or a different business.

Two refinements matter. The RM999 Billplz plan pays for itself at about **2,000 FPX orders a year**, where the RM0.50 saved per transaction covers the annual fee. And when the commission on a basket is smaller than the flat FPX fee, no order count breaks even — a result worth having before building a checkout.

The identity behind these rows is derived in [The commission arithmetic behind owning your shop](https://upahje.my/en/articles/commission-arithmetic): this page states the result, that one shows the algebra.

## The licence stack comes first, and it is mostly unpriced

Before the fee arithmetic, there is an opening bill. Two councils print itemised fees, and both are narrower than readers assume. MBSA prints application processing at RM50.00 for a business and RM100.00 for industrial, a licence plate at RM7.00 and a sticker at RM5.00 — none of which is the annual premises licence. MBPJ prints signboard fees: RM100.00 a year non-illuminated up to 8 m², RM10.00 for each extra square metre, RM150.00 illuminated with RM15.00 extra, RM200.00 for a logo-type advertisement, RM20.00 per additional logo unit, and a RM25.00 administration charge.

Business registration is the other priced part: RM60 a year for a trade name, RM30 for a personal name, RM5 per branch, or RM1,000 to incorporate a Sdn Bhd.

Three licence categories remain unpriced in everything read for this series: the annual premises licence (DBKL's schedule sits behind its e-lesen portal, MBSJ prints none), food-handler certification (no provider page read) and halal certification (not attempted). An opening budget that treats those as zero is not a budget.

## Delivery is where the comparison usually turns

The commission covers a rider. Your own checkout does not, so the delivery comparison is not the same as the pickup comparison. On a RM30 order, a rider you arrange at the RM5.00 to RM8.00 this series uses as an assumption leaves you RM20.75 to RM23.75, against RM24.00 kept through a platform at 20%, a point inside the regulator's 15% to 25% band.

Five courier services were checked for this series and none publishes a per-km tariff: GrabExpress renders with only two add-on prices, RM0.80 and RM1.30, which are add-ons and not a fare; Lalamove's pricing page 404s and the fare needs its configurator; Pos Laju renders client-side; J&T and Ninja Van return 404. The radius where your own rider wins is therefore arithmetic from your own quotes, not a published rate.

## Discovery is the job you inherit

The platform's commission was buying customers as well as software, and when you own the storefront that purchase stops. The break-even equation above carries the marketing term for exactly this reason, and no Malaysian rate exists for it.

What exists is the example. mejamakan, a lauk delivery business in KL and Selangor, runs its own site with a weekly menu, accounts and ordering on its own domain, "from RM18/meal", weekday delivery and a renewing plan, with Instagram at 16K followers as the front door. The followers are the observable part and also the limit of the example: followers are not orders, and no conversion rate is published.

The measure that matters is the **share of orders arriving through a channel you own**. Below half, the platform is still buying your customers and its commission is the cheaper way to get them. Above half, it is a delivery contractor and the commission conversation changes.

## What to do, in the order a reader meets it

1. Write down your three distances, your average basket and your orders a month.
2. Get the payment rate for the method you will actually use: FPX flat, card percentage, settlement per method.
3. Get the commission from your own contract in writing, and use the regulator's n=5 bands only until then.
4. Put the numbers into the equation and read the order count, with the marketing term included.
5. Get the licence stack priced before signing a lease, because the annual premises fee is not on any page read here.
6. Count the share of your orders from a channel you own, and watch that number monthly.

## The two mistakes this arithmetic prevents

The first is buying a checkout for a shop with no audience. The build is cheap and the fee is a ringgit, so the purchase feels small, but the equation shows the trap: with the marketing term unfunded, the owned side's cost is a fixed bill and no orders, and the break-even count is not a number the shop can reach. The checkout is not the mistake; buying it before there is anyone to check out is.

The second is staying on a platform whose commission sits below the payment fee. The table's bottom rows make it visible: at the low end of the regulator's band, the commission on a small basket can be smaller than a flat FPX fee, and then no order count breaks even on owning. In that range, the platform is genuinely cheaper and the argument for leaving it is about control and customer data, not about money.

Both mistakes come from the same habit: comparing a percentage with a percentage when one side is flat, or comparing a build cost with a commission without counting the discovery the commission included.

## Four numbers nobody publishes

No platform publishes a merchant commission. No Malaysian page prints a cost of acquiring a food customer. No courier publishes a per-km tariff. Three of the six gateways read for this series will not quote a rate without a conversation: senangPay prints a RM199-a-year platform fee and no MDR, iPay88's pricing page is 404, and toyyibPay serves JavaScript shells.

Those absences are not a reason to guess. They are the reason the pieces in this series are methods with a few published numbers inside them, rather than rate cards pretending to be advice. A reader who substitutes their own contract, their own quotes and their own wage gets an answer that applies to their shop. A reader who takes a remembered percentage from a blog gets a number that was never theirs.

Owning the shop is worth doing when the commission is high, the basket is large, the delivery radius is short and you can build an audience. It is worth delaying when the opposite is true. The arithmetic is the only way to know which shop you have.
