# Before your first freelance client: the boring checklist

The work is the fun part. The part that decides whether the second year is easier than the first is a small set of administrative decisions you can make in one afternoon — before a client is involved. Here they are, in the order that causes the least rework.

## What should you settle before taking a client?

Decide the four things a client will ask about in the first ten minutes: what you do, what it costs, how long it takes, and how you get paid. If any of those four answers changes per client, that is fine — but you need a default.

## What is the checklist?

1. **Pick the trading name and check whether registration applies.** If you will invoice under a business name rather than your own full name, look up the current SSM requirement before you print anything.
2. **Open a separate bank account.** Every ringgit in and out of the freelance work goes through it. It takes minutes and it makes the year-end trivial.
3. **Set a floor rate.** Work out your monthly floor, divide by the billable hours you can actually sell (not all your waking hours), and that is your break-even. Quote above it.
4. **Write a one-page scope template.** Deliverables, revisions, timeline, payment schedule, what is not included. Reuse it every time.
5. **Make an invoice template.** Invoice number, date, your details, the client's, the deliverable, the amount, the payment terms, and a due date.
6. **Start a records folder.** Every invoice, every receipt, every bank statement. One folder and one spreadsheet beat a shoebox in April.
7. **Read the current LHDN guidance for your situation.** If you already have employment income, the interaction with your freelance income is worth one careful read rather than a guess.

## How do you set a rate you can defend?

Start from what the work needs to earn, not from what a competitor charges. Add the hours of unpaid work — quoting, revisions, travel, invoicing — to the hours on the job. Then add your costs: software, equipment, transport, insurance if you carry it.

A rate built this way survives the question "why does it cost that much?" because the answer is a list, not a feeling. It also tells you which jobs to decline, which is the skill that keeps a side income from turning into a second unpaid job.

## What do clients expect to see on an invoice?

At minimum: who is billing, who is being billed, what was delivered, how much, when it is due, and how to pay it. Add your registration number if you have one. If the client is a company, ask for their details before the first invoice so the accounts team does not bounce it.

## What it takes

An afternoon, a folder, and a template or two. The registration and account steps depend on your situation and the current rules at SSM; the rate and template steps are entirely yours. The cost of skipping this is not dramatic — it is just an annual scramble, unpaid tax you did not plan for, and arguments about scope you cannot settle because nothing was written down.

## FAQ

### Do I need an SSM registration to freelance?

It depends on how you trade and under what name. If you invoice under a business name, registration is generally expected; working under your own name as an individual is treated differently. Check the current SSM guidance for your specific case before spending on printing or a domain.

### Can I freelance while employed full-time?

Often yes, but read your employment contract first. Some contracts restrict outside work or claim intellectual property created for other parties. Settle that question before your first client, not after.

### How much tax should I set aside?

There is no single percentage that fits everyone, because it depends on your other income and reliefs. A common working habit is to move a fixed share of every payment into a separate tax account as soon as it arrives, then reconcile with LHDN's published rates. Confirm the rate that applies to you rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

### Do I need insurance?

If you work on client premises or handle expensive equipment, third-party cover is worth pricing. At minimum, check whether your existing home or vehicle insurance covers gear used for work; often it does not.
